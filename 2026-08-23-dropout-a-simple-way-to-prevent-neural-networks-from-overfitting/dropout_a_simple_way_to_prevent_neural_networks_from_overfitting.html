Regularization · Srivastava et al.

Learn without fragile allies.

Dropout makes a network practice with different internal routes missing on every training pass. A feature that only works beside one particular companion becomes unreliable; useful features learn to survive many contexts.

01 Overfitting can be a teamwork problem

A large network can fit training examples by developing feature partnerships that are useful only together: “I will detect this pattern because that other unit always fixes my mistake.” This is co-adaptation. It can make training loss small while leaving the representation brittle when data changes.

At training

Sample a mask. Dropped units and their incident connections are temporarily absent for this pass.

Across passes

Sample another mask. Many thinned subnetworks share the same underlying weights rather than being independently trained models.

At test

Use one full network with an expectation-matched scaling convention; do not literally run and save every possible subnetwork.

Prerequisite bridge. Dropout does not permanently delete neurons, delete data, or choose an optimizer. It changes the stochastic network and gradients exposed during each training pass.

03 Why scale at test time?

Let h=[2,4], w=[3,1], and retention p=0.5. The full unscaled preactivation is z=3·2+1·4=10. Under the original convention, individual training masks create noisy values; the deterministic test network should match their expected linear output, not one particular mask.

m=[1,0] → z=6    m=[0,1] → z=4
E[z]=0.5·6+0.5·4=5
ztest=wT(p·h)=3·1+1·2=5

Predict before reveal: preserve the expectation

With retention p=0.5, should the original paper’s test network multiply outgoing weights by 1, p, or 1/p?

Static answer: p. A retained training activation has expected value p·h. Scaling weights (or equivalently activations) by p at test time matches that expectation in a linear preactivation.

Implementation note: inverted dropout performs the compensating 1/p rescaling during training instead, so its ordinary test network needs no additional p scaling. Do not mix the two conventions.

04 A missing route receives no gradient

Continue with target t=5 and squared loss L=½(z−t)². With m=[1,0], the training preactivation is z=6. The retained first route participates; the dropped second route does not.

∂L/∂w1=(z−t)·h̃1=(6−5)·2=2
∂L/∂w2=(z−t)·h̃2=(6−5)·0=0

For the alternate mask m=[0,1], z=4, so ∂L/∂w1=0 and ∂L/∂w2=(4−5)·4=−4. Different passes force different routes to carry useful evidence. That is the training pressure behind “do not co-adapt too much.”

Misconception: “Dropout means no unit can rely on any other unit.”

No. It makes particular fixed dependencies less reliable during training; networks can still learn distributed, complementary representations. The mechanism is stochastic regularization, not a ban on feature interaction.

Misconception: “Dropout and BatchNorm are the same kind of train/test difference.”

No. BatchNorm estimates and applies feature statistics, using batch statistics during training and stored estimates at inference. Dropout samples missing activation routes during training and uses an expectation-matched full network at standard inference.

05 Read the evidence with its scope

The paper evaluates supervised classification across vision, speech, text, and computational biology. Its datasets include MNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet, TIMIT, Reuters-RCV1, and alternative splicing. Its headline empirical claim is broad reduction of overfitting and improvements over non-dropout networks across its reported settings—not a proof that one retention rate or placement wins everywhere.

Specific reported result

For the stated SVHN convolutional-network comparison, the paper reports 3.95% error without dropout, 3.02% when applying it only to fully connected layers, and 2.55% when also applying it to convolutional layers.

Specific reported practice

The paper says retaining hidden units with probability 0.5 was often effective, while input-unit retention was usually closer to 1. These are paper-era empirical choices, not universal defaults.

Limit. The test-time scaled network is an approximation to averaging the thinned networks. Nonlinearities, architecture, data size, placement, and optimization all affect whether dropout helps and how much.

07 Connect it to your mental map

Transformer
Attention creates rich dependencies between token features. Dropout can regularize those internal representations during training, while attention itself still decides what information can communicate.
ResNet
Residual shortcuts preserve a dependable information route. Dropout temporarily removes selected learned activation routes during training; these are opposite-looking tools aimed at different robustness problems.
DQN
Replay reduces correlation among transition samples and the target network steadies bootstrapped labels. Dropout perturbs feature routes. Both add useful stochastic discipline, but neither replaces the other.
GAN
Dropout can regularize a component, but it does not solve the coupled, non-stationary generator-discriminator game or mode collapse.
Adam
Adam calibrates how parameters move; dropout changes which thinned model and gradients Adam sees on each pass.

Takeaway

Native intuition: dropout is rehearsal under missing teammates. Each training pass removes a different set of internal routes, so a useful representation cannot depend on one fragile coalition. The full test network then approximates the average behavior of those shared-weight rehearsals.
