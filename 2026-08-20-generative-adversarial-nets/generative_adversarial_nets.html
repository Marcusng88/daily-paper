A generator learns through a moving test.

A GAN does not receive a fixed checklist for looking real. A generator proposes samples; a discriminator discovers the current differences from data; gradients through that learned test tell the generator what to change.

01 The object to learn is a distribution

A classifier can succeed by drawing a boundary: “this input is a cat.” A generator has a broader responsibility: put probability mass wherever real examples occur, across their variations, and avoid impossible regions. A city map analogy is useful: matching one beautiful plaza is not the same as learning where the population lives.

Real data, pdata

The unknown process producing genuine examples x.

Noise prior, pz

An easy random source such as a Gaussian. Each z supplies a different starting coordinate.

Generated distribution, pg

The distribution created when G maps z into data space.

Prerequisite bridge. An average face is not a face distribution. A successful generator must capture identity, pose, lighting, and other modes—not just produce something near the average.

The original GAN is an implicit generative model: it readily samples x=G(z), but does not explicitly provide a tractable pg(x). That avoids direct likelihood evaluation, while making evaluation and training dynamics more subtle.

02 Make mismatch into a differentiable test

The paper’s counterfeit analogy is exact enough to be useful. A counterfeiter G makes candidate notes; an inspector D sees genuine and counterfeit notes and estimates which source produced each. The inspector’s current distinction becomes the counterfeiter’s feedback.

  1. Update D. Show real x with target 1 and generated G(z) with target 0; adjust θd to distinguish sources.
  2. Hold D’s parameters fixed. Draw new noise and evaluate its generated samples with the current D.
  3. Update G. Backpropagate through D’s computation to adjust θg so generated samples earn a more-real judgment.
Role check. D(x) is not an absolute quality score. It is a learned, changing source judgment relative to the current generator and training data.

Worked step A: the optimal discriminator

Freeze G. At one location x, write a=pdata(x), b=pg(x), and let y=D(x). D chooses y to maximize a log y+b log(1−y).

d/dy[a log y + b log(1−y)] = a/y − b/(1−y) = 0 a(1−y)=by → y=a/(a+b) D*(x)=pdata(x) / [pdata(x)+pg(x)]

The discriminator is estimating a density ratio. If real density is 0.30 and generated density is 0.10 at a location, D*(x)=0.30/0.40=0.75. “0.75” means locally three times as much real as generated mass—not “75% visual quality.”

Predict before reveal: a density-ratio judgment

At a location with pdata(x)=0.2 and pg(x)=0.8, should D*(x) be above or below 1/2?

Below 1/2. D*(x)=0.2/(0.2+0.8)=0.2. The generator has overfilled this region, so a sample there is more likely generated.

Static answer: D*(x)=0.2.

Worked step B: a two-bin collapse

Let real data be split evenly over L and R: pdata(L)=pdata(R)=0.5. Let a collapsed generator put all mass on L: pg(L)=1, pg(R)=0.

D*(L)=0.5/(0.5+1)=1/3 D*(R)=0.5/(0.5+0)=1 L is over-produced; R is entirely missing from G.

Now substitute D* into the value function. The paper obtains C(G)=−log 4+2·JSD(pdata||pg). Jensen–Shannon divergence is non-negative and is zero only when distributions match. At that match, D*=1/2 everywhere and C(G)=log(1/2)+log(1/2)=−log 4.

04 Practical training: the gradient matters

The literal minimax generator minimizes log(1−D(G(z))). Early in training, D often calls generated samples fake with near certainty. The paper notes that this term can saturate, offering too little useful gradient. It recommends instead maximizing log D(G(z)). Both objectives share the intended fixed point, but they behave differently while learning.

Literal minimax loss

If D(G(z))=0.01, log(1−0.01)=log(0.99)≈−0.010: the outer loss is already near zero.

Non-saturating generator loss

For the same judgment, log(0.01)≈−4.605: strongly encourage G to increase D(G(z)).

Worked step C: the update rhythm

  1. Sample m noise vectors and m real examples.
  2. Take k discriminator ascent steps on log D(real)+log(1−D(fake)). The paper used k=1 in its experiments.
  3. Sample new noise and update G through D. In practice use the non-saturating objective when the minimax generator loss saturates.
Scope of the theorem. The convergence result assumes an optimal discriminator, sufficient capacity, and suitably small distribution-level updates. Alternating stochastic neural-network updates only approximate those conditions.

05 Evidence, limits, and diagnostics

The paper trained multilayer-perceptron GANs on MNIST, Toronto Face Database, and CIFAR-10, and also showed a convolutional discriminator with a “deconvolutional” generator. Figure 1 is the clearest visual argument: it shows D becoming a better separator, G shifting mass toward data, and the ideal state where source is indistinguishable.

Mode collapse

Generated samples look plausible but cover too few modes. Quality and coverage are separate requirements.

Discriminator saturation

D separates sources too easily, making its feedback weak under the literal minimax generator loss.

Moving-target dynamics

G changes D’s task; D changes G’s loss. A single loss curve does not guarantee stable progress.

Table 1 reports Parzen-window likelihood estimates, and the paper itself warns that this estimator has high variance and performs poorly in high dimensions. Treat it as historical evidence of feasibility, not as a definitive modern evaluation. The framework’s advantages include sampling by forward propagation and no Markov chains or inference during generation; its disadvantages include no explicit pg(x) and the need to keep D synchronized with G.

Predict before reveal: realism versus coverage

A generator produces one extremely convincing face for every noise seed. A classifier cannot distinguish that face from real faces. Did it match the face distribution?

No. It could still have zero diversity across seeds. It is mode collapse: good-looking local samples, poor coverage of the distribution.

Static answer: no; distribution matching requires both fidelity and coverage.

06 Retrieve, then connect

Why is D*(x)=1/2 at the ideal match?

When pg=pdata, x gives no evidence about the source. The density ratio is p/(p+p)=1/2.

Which model sees real images during its direct update?

The discriminator. The generator learns through gradients from D’s judgment of generated samples.

What changes with the non-saturating loss?

The target equilibrium stays the same, but G receives stronger early learning pressure when D confidently rejects generated samples.

Are GANs the same as adversarial examples?

No. Adversarial examples optimize an input to fool a fixed classifier; GANs train a generator through a jointly learned discriminator.

Connect it to your mental map

GANs join the Transformer, ResNet, and DQN as papers about creating useful learning signals. Transformers use content-dependent context; ResNets make refinement easy through an identity path; DQN bootstraps from a delayed moving target. GANs use a learned critic whose own discrimination changes as the generator changes. The common question is: what feedback is the model following, and when does it remain informative?

Transformer

Content-dependent attention tells each token which context matters.

ResNet

Residual paths make learned changes additive refinements.

DQN

Bootstrapped targets introduce moving feedback.

GAN

A critic learns the current mismatch and differentiates it back to G.

Takeaway

Native intuition: a GAN is distribution matching through an adaptive test. The discriminator identifies where generated samples still reveal their origin; the generator changes until the test has no source evidence left. This makes GANs flexible—and fragile—because the teacher and learner move together.
