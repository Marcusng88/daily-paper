Srivastava et al. · JMLR 2014

Learn when routes disappear

Dropout trains one shared network under many temporary absences. The causal story is simple: unreliable partners disrupt brittle co-adaptation, masked routes redirect learning, and one scaled full network stands in for a huge shared-weight ensemble at inference.

Prerequisite bridge0-7 min

From one dependable layer to a team with absences

What must already make sense before a missing neuron can make sense?

A feed-forward layer receives a vector, multiplies it by a weight matrix, adds a bias, and applies a nonlinearity. Backpropagation then sends credit or blame only through the computation that actually happened. Overfitting appears when low training error does not carry over to unseen examples.

Layer

y(l) is the activation vector leaving layer l. It is the information available to the next layer.

Gradient route

If a quantity does not participate in the forward computation, that route contributes zero to its gradient on that pass.

Generalization gap

A widening gap between training and held-out performance signals that the learned solution may depend on quirks of the sample.

Analogy - a rotating study group. If the same four students always solve every problem together, one may learn only to finish another's partial argument. Now make one or two members absent at random. Each student has to produce an explanation that remains useful beside many possible partners. Dropout imposes that rotating attendance policy on hidden units.

The analogy has a boundary: units are not people, and dropout does not guarantee independent features. It makes a particular fixed coalition unreliable during training; useful cooperation can still emerge.

The problem7-15 min

Overfitting can be a coordination failure

Why can a network with enough capacity fit perfectly and still fail?

In the paper's account, ordinary backpropagation lets each unit update while every other unit is present. A unit can learn a fragile feature because another specific unit reliably repairs its mistakes. Those tightly coupled feature partnerships are co-adaptations.

Evidence boundary. Co-adaptation is the paper's main explanatory hypothesis, supported by qualitative feature analyses and broad task results. The experiments do not prove it is the only reason dropout helps.

Source mechanism19-22 min

One parent network, many thinned networks

With n droppable units there are 2n possible binary masks. The paper's key efficiency move is weight sharing: these subnetworks are views of one parameter set, not 2n separately stored and fully trained models.

The intervention22-25 min

Sample a mask, then train only the surviving computation

For each training case, the paper samples a fresh thinned network. Forward propagation and backpropagation occur only through that subnetwork. A parameter unused by that case contributes a gradient of zero for that case; gradients are then averaged over the mini-batch.

Worked trace II31-35 min

Gradient: a missing route receives zero credit and zero blame

Continue with target t=5 and squared loss L=12(z−t)2. For mask m=[10], the preactivation is z=6.

Mask [1,0] ∂L∂w1 = (z−t)h~1=(6−5)·2=2 ∂L∂w2 = (z−t)h~2=(6−5)·0=0

With learning rate η=0.1, gradient descent gives

w1←3−0.1·2=2.8w2←1

while w2 stays at 1 on this pass.

Now switch to mask m=[01]. Then

z=4∂L∂w1=0∂L∂w2=(4−5)·4=−4

The second route now has to correct the error without help from the first.

Causal consequence. Masks do more than add output noise. They alter which parameter routes participate in each example's gradient, repeatedly changing the context in which a feature must be useful.
Worked trace III35-40 min

Inference: two scaling conventions, one rule - never mix them

The paper describes unscaled retained activations during training and multiplies outgoing weights by p at test time. Many modern libraries use inverted dropout: divide retained activations by p during training, then do nothing at test time.

The paper states these forms are equivalent with appropriate scaling of learning rates and weight initializations. The linear expectation match is exact; replacing an ensemble's full nonlinear predictive distribution with one scaled network remains an approximation.

Interpretation40-43 min

What the update is really doing

Each mask selects a thinned architecture, yet all masks write into the same underlying weights. Over many cases, a unit is optimized under many neighborhoods. This creates two linked interpretations:

Regularization

Random absence makes exact co-adaptations unreliable and pushes features to remain useful in varied contexts.

Noise injection

Bernoulli multiplicative noise changes activations and the gradient paths seen on every case.

Model combination

Training samples exponentially many shared-weight subnetworks; deterministic scaling approximates their combined prediction.

These are not three unrelated stories. The shared cause is the mask: it perturbs representation learning during training and motivates averaging behavior at test time.

Read the comparison narrowly. Table 4 reports these two dataset-specific errors for the paper's tuned network and training recipe. It does not show that every placement rate or every modern architecture benefits from applying dropout to every layer.

The paper also reports improvements across MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet, TIMIT, Reuters-RCV1, and alternative splicing. Read these as results for the paper's tuned architectures and training recipes, not as a guarantee for every modern model or placement.

Limits and diagnostics50-54 min

Useful regularization has a range, not a universal setting

The paper itself shows important boundaries. In its MNIST data-size study, dropout did not improve the extremely small 100- and 500-example settings. When p is too small while width is fixed, too few units survive and the network underfits. On the large Reuters subset, the improvement was less significant because overfitting was already less serious.

Paper-era heuristic, not law. Retaining hidden units with p=0.5 was often effective in these experiments; input retention was usually closer to 1. Architecture, layer type, data size, and optimization all change the best choice.
Misconception: “Dropout permanently removes neurons.”

No. Standard dropout removes activations and their incident computation only for a sampled training pass. The full network is available at standard inference.

Misconception: “p means probability of dropping a unit.”

Not in this paper. Here p is the probability of retaining a unit. The drop probability is 1−p. Some APIs name their argument the other way around, so always check the convention.

Misconception: “Every one of the 2n subnetworks is trained to completion.”

No. A new mask samples one shared-weight subnetwork for a case. Many possible subnetworks may never occur, and none owns a separate parameter copy.

Misconception: “Weight scaling exactly averages every nonlinear prediction.”

No. It exactly matches the expected linear input under independent masks. The single scaled nonlinear network is an efficient approximation to model averaging.

Misconception: “Dropout and BatchNorm create the same train-test difference.”

No. Dropout samples missing activation routes and then removes that randomness at standard inference. BatchNorm normalizes with mini-batch statistics during training and stored estimates at inference.

Open the answer key
  1. The Bernoulli mask r(l); elementwise multiplication requires it to match y(l).
  2. Retention probability. A mask entry is 1 with probability p.
  3. Its masked activation is zero, so the forward computation does not depend on that route and its local gradient contribution is zero.
  4. Because E[r⊙y]=py; scaling matches the expected linear signal produced under the training masks.
  5. All thinned subnetworks share one underlying set of weights; the mask chooses which subset participates.
  6. Examples: it did not improve the 100- or 500-example MNIST cases in the paper; too-small p with fixed width underfit; the Reuters gain was less significant when overfitting was less serious.
Connect it to your mental map58-60 min

Place dropout among the things that change learning

Adam
Adam changes how a gradient moves parameters. Dropout changes which subnetwork produces that gradient. They act on different parts of the training loop.
BatchNorm
BatchNorm uses batch-derived activation statistics; dropout uses Bernoulli gates. Both distinguish training from inference, but for different reasons and with different state.
ResNet
A residual shortcut provides a reliable path for information and gradients. Dropout deliberately makes selected learned paths unreliable during training.
Transformer
Attention creates content-dependent communication routes. Dropout may regularize activations or attention-related quantities, but it does not decide which tokens are relevant.
Ensembling
A conventional ensemble trains separate parameter sets. Dropout samples many architectures with extensive weight sharing and approximates their combination with one test network.
TakeawayKeep this sentence

Rehearse under missing teammates

Dropout samples temporary missing routes during training, so features must remain useful across many partner sets; shared weights learn from those thinned networks, and an expectation-matched full network provides efficient deterministic inference.
