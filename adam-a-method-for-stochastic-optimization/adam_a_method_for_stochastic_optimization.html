Optimization field lesson · 60 minutes

Learning to step on uneven ground

Adam gives every parameter two memories: one for where gradients have pointed, and one for how large they have been. Their ratio turns one global learning rate into coordinate-wise steps.

Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization · Diederik P. Kingma and Jimmy Lei Ba · ICLR 2015

01 · The problem

One step size meets a warped landscape

A gradient is a local arrow: it points uphill, so an optimizer moves against it. The learning rate decides the length of that move. Plain SGD uses the same length rule for every parameter—even when the surface is steep in one coordinate and shallow in another.

Intuition bridge. Imagine descending a foggy mountain while feeling the slope only under each boot. On a narrow ridge, an east-west tilt may be one hundred times stronger than the north-south tilt. A stride safe in the steep direction barely moves you along the gentle one; a stride useful along the gentle one can throw you across the ridge.

A failed baseline, with numbers

Use the two-dimensional bowl L(x,y)=12(100x2+y2). Its gradient is ∇L=[100xy]. At θ₀=(1,1), the two coordinates report gradients on very different scales.

Useful for y, unstable for x

α=0.02 gives θ1=(−10.98).

The next x-gradient changes from +100 to −100, so x returns to +1. It ping-pongs while y crawls.

Safe for x, slow for y

α=0.005 gives θ1=(0.50.995).

The steep coordinate behaves, but the shallow coordinate moves only 0.005 toward zero.

This toy does not prove Adam wins on every loss. It isolates the design pressure: a useful optimizer should remember direction while calibrating scale separately for each coordinate.

03 · Mechanism

Two memories, then one calibrated step

Adam combines momentum-like direction memory with RMS-style scale memory. The causal chain matters: observe a gradient, update both ledgers, repair their zero-start bias, then divide.

mt=β1mt−1+(1−β1)gt vt=β2vt−1+(1−β2)gt2 m^t=mt1−β1t v^t=vt1−β2t θt=θt−1−α·m^tv^t+ε

All products, squares, square roots, and divisions involving vectors are elementwise. No matrix inverse appears; coordinate i uses only the history stored for coordinate i.

Why the hats are necessary

Both memories begin at zero. If the mean gradient stayed at μ, then the expected first ledger after t steps would be (1−β1t)μ, not μ. Dividing by 1−β1t removes that initialization shrinkage; the same logic applies to the second raw moment.

Default settings in Algorithm 1. For the tested problems, the paper gives α=0.001, β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999, and ε=10⁻⁸ as good defaults. “Good defaults” is an empirical starting point, not a universal optimum.
04 · Work it

Two update traces, fully opened

Use the paper's default decay rates, α=0.001, and m₀=v₀=0. We omit ε only where every denominator is safely nonzero, so the arithmetic exposes the mechanism.

Worked trace A

One coordinate meets a reversal

The gradient is +4 at step 1 and −2 at step 2. Will one opposing observation instantly reverse the update?

t = 1
m1=0.9(0)+0.1(4)=0.4 v1=0.999(0)+0.001(42)=0.016 m^1=0.40.1=4 v^1=0.0160.001=16 Δθ1=−0.001·416=−0.001
t = 2
m2=0.9(0.4)+0.1(−2)=0.16 v2=0.999(0.016)+0.001(−2)2=0.019984 m^2=0.161−0.92=0.842 v^2=0.0199841−0.9992=9.997 Δθ2≈−0.001·0.8429.997=−0.000266

The new gradient is negative, but the signed history remains positive. Adam therefore continues in the earlier descent direction with a much smaller update. Momentum is doing exactly what it was designed to do: resist one noisy reversal.

Worked trace B

Two coordinates carry different histories

Now let g1=[100.1] and g2=[−20.1]. The first coordinate starts one hundred times larger and then reverses; the second stays small and consistent.

t = 1
m1=[10.01] v1=[0.10.00001] m^1=[100.1] v^1=[1000.01] Δθ1=−0.001·[10100.10.1]=[−0.001−0.001]

At the first step, scale cancels: both nonzero positive coordinates move by about α. At the second, the large coordinate's reversal weakens its signed memory while its scale memory remains high, so it receives about half a base step. The small consistent coordinate receives a full one.

05 · Instrument

Change the second gradient before revealing the answer

The first observation is fixed at g1=[100.1]. Predict what happens after g2=[−20.1]: which coordinate receives the larger magnitude update—x, y, or neither?

Reveal the prediction answer

y receives the larger update. The x-gradient reversed, so its signed first moment weakens, while its second-moment scale remains large. The y-gradient stayed small but consistent, so its corrected numerator and denominator still cancel to a ratio near 1.

06 · Paper evidence

Read the result before naming a winner

Figure 3 tests training cost on one CIFAR-10 convolutional network. Its two panels answer different questions: what happens in the first three passes through the data, and what happens over 45 passes?

Check the axes

Horizontal position counts iterations over the entire dataset. Vertical position is training cost, not validation accuracy.

Separate the time scales

The left panel is only 0-3 epochs with a linear y-axis. Adam and Adam+dropout make rapid early progress; that is a claim about the start.

Notice the log scale

The right panel spans 45 epochs and powers of ten. Adam and SGD with Nesterov finish far below AdaGrad; equal vertical gaps represent multiplicative, not additive, differences.

The authors' own interpretation is careful: Adam and AdaGrad both lower cost rapidly at first, but Adam and SGD eventually converge considerably faster than AdaGrad on this CNN. Adam shows a marginal improvement over momentum SGD while adapting learning-rate scales across layers instead of hand-picking them.

Sharp but scoped conclusion. The figure supports a training-optimization claim for this architecture, data, initialization protocol, hyperparameter search, and cost. It does not establish that Adam always generalizes better, reaches the best test accuracy, or beats every later optimizer.
07 · Boundaries

What Adam buys—and what it cannot know

What the mechanism buys

First-order computation, two state vectors, coordinate-wise scaling, resistance to isolated sign changes, and a correction for zero-initialized moving averages.

What remains outside it

Full cross-coordinate curvature, the best global schedule, validation performance, and guarantees for arbitrary non-convex neural-network objectives.

The paper's convergence analysis is framed in online convex optimization under bounded-gradient, bounded-distance, learning-rate-decay, and first-moment-decay assumptions. The CNN experiment is non-convex empirical evidence; it is not covered by that convex guarantee.

The paper also reports a revealing weakness in this CNN experiment: after a few epochs, its second-moment estimate becomes very small and ε dominates, so v̂ is a poor approximation to the cost geometry. The authors attribute more of the speed-up there to the first moment reducing minibatch variance.

Misconception checks

“vₜ is the variance of the gradients.”

No. It is an exponential average of uncentered squared gradients—a second raw moment. Adam does not subtract the mean before squaring.

“Bias correction fixes biased minibatch gradients.”

No. It corrects the predictable shrinkage of moment estimates caused by initializing their exponential averages at zero.

“Adam learns the Hessian.”

No. Its denominator is one historical scale per coordinate. It cannot represent rotations or interactions between coordinates as a full Hessian could.

“Gradient-scale invariance is exact even when ε matters.”

No. Rescaling g rescales m̂ by the same factor and v̂ by its square, so they cancel when ε is negligible. A fixed ε breaks exact cancellation near zero.

“The defaults are laws.”

No. Algorithm 1 calls them good defaults for the tested machine-learning problems. Data, architecture, regularization, schedule, and objective can change the useful settings.

08 · Active recall

Close the loop without looking back

Answer each aloud before opening it. Every answer should follow from the terrain, ledgers, traces, or evidence you just used.

Why can one learning rate be awkward in a narrow valley?

A step large enough to move along a shallow coordinate may overshoot the steep coordinate; a step safe for the steep coordinate can make painfully slow progress along the shallow one.

What information is kept by mₜ but destroyed by vₜ?

The sign. mt averages signed gradients and therefore remembers direction; vt squares first and remembers scale without sign.

Why divide by 1−βᵗ?

A zero-initialized exponential average contains only a fraction 1−βt of a stationary signal after t steps. The division removes that initialization shrinkage.

At the first step, why did gradients 10 and 0.1 both produce an update near α?

Bias correction gave m^=g and v^=g2. With negligible ε, gg2=g|g|, whose magnitude is 1 for each nonzero coordinate.

Why did the x update shrink after its gradient reversed?

The positive first-step direction still contributed to m^2, while the squared large gradient still contributed strongly to v^2. The numerator weakened but the denominator stayed large.

What exactly does Figure 3 establish?

For the displayed CIFAR-10 CNN experiment, Adam and AdaGrad both progressed rapidly at first, while Adam and momentum SGD eventually reached much lower training cost than AdaGrad. It does not establish universal test accuracy or optimizer dominance.

09 · Mental map

Connect it to your mental map

Adam belongs to the optimization branch: it changes how parameters move, not what the model computes or what the objective asks for.

BatchNorm
BatchNorm controls activation statistics inside a network. Adam controls parameter-update scale across time. Normalized features are not normalized optimizer steps.
Dropout
Dropout injects stochastic masks to regularize a model. That makes the objective noisier; Adam consumes the resulting gradients but does not replace regularization.
Transformer
Attention defines information flow. Adam may train its weights, while the Transformer's learning-rate schedule remains a separate design choice.
VAE
The reparameterization trick makes stochastic latent-variable objectives differentiable. Those gradients still need an optimizer; Adam turns them into parameter updates.
DPO
DPO defines a preference loss. Backpropagation produces its gradients; Adam can then smooth and scale those gradients coordinate by coordinate.

Takeaway

Native intuition: Adam walks with two ledgers beside every parameter—signed direction above, squared scale below. Bias correction makes the empty ledgers honest at the start. Their ratio is a measured next step on uneven terrain, not a map of the whole mountain.
