One-hour paper lesson

Proximal policy optimization

How can a policy learn from a promising batch of experience without trusting that batch enough to take a ruinous leap? PPO clips the incentive to move too far.

Route: policy versus value · probability ratios · sign-sensitive clipping · two numerical traces · benchmark evidence · limits and recall

01 · The promise

Improve an action distribution, cautiously.

DQN asks which action has the highest estimated value. A policy-gradient agent instead directly changes a distribution: in this state, make helpful actions more probable and harmful ones less probable. That directness is useful for continuous actions, but it creates a risk. A noisy estimate from one rollout can push the new policy far away from the policy that collected the data.

PPO alternates two jobs: collect trajectories with an old policy, then make several minibatch passes over that fixed batch. Its key contribution is a first-order objective that stops giving extra credit once a probability change has become sufficiently large in the “helpful” direction.

02 · Bridge

A thermostat should correct, not overcorrect.

Imagine adjusting a thermostat after a chilly hour. Turning it slightly upward responds to the evidence. Turning it to maximum because of one cold reading may overshoot badly. The old policy is the calibrated thermostat; the sampled advantage says which way to turn; clipping says the one reading cannot keep rewarding a larger turn forever.

PPO is not “clipping gradients.”

It clips the probability ratio inside the objective. Gradients arise afterwards from differentiating that objective.

At the start of optimization, the policies match and r = 1. If r = 1.3, the proposed policy assigns the sampled action 30% more probability than the old policy did.

04 · The clipped surrogate

Keep the smaller of two stories about the gain.

LCLIP(θ)=E^t[min(rtA^t,clip(rt,1−ε,1+ε)A^t)]

The min is crucial, and the sign of the advantage changes which side becomes pessimistic. For a positive advantage, PPO stops rewarding r above 1 + ε. For a negative advantage, it stops rewarding r below 1 − ε; making a bad action dramatically less likely would otherwise make its negative contribution look artificially good.

05 · Worked trace A

A good action: cap the reward for overconfidence.

Let Â = +2, ε = 0.2, and r = 1.4. The ordinary surrogate gives 1.4 × 2 = 2.8. Clipping r to 1.2 gives 1.2 × 2 = 2.4. PPO chooses min(2.8, 2.4) = 2.4. The action can become more likely, but r = 1.4 earns no additional objective credit over r = 1.2.

Predict. With the same positive advantage, if r = 0.6, is the contribution clipped upward?

No. The ordinary value 0.6 × 2 = 1.2 is already the smaller term; PPO keeps it. Clipping does not protect a policy from a change that makes the sampled good action less likely—it only removes incentive to move too far in the apparently beneficial direction.

06 · Worked trace B

A bad action: the direction flips.

Let Â = −3, ε = 0.2, and r = 0.5. Ordinary: 0.5 × −3 = −1.5. Clipped: 0.8 × −3 = −2.4. PPO chooses min(−1.5, −2.4) = −2.4. A huge decrease in the probability of a bad sampled action is not allowed to improve the surrogate beyond the clip boundary.

For Â = +2.0 and r = 1.4, the clipped contribution is 2.4 rather than the ordinary 2.8.

Predict before moving the sliders: when Â is negative, which boundary should make the contribution flat—1 − ε or 1 + ε?

1 − ε. A negative advantage supports making the sampled action less likely, but the objective stops rewarding that decrease once r falls below 1 − ε.

07 · From trajectory to advantage

The critic supplies a baseline, not a verdict.

A trajectory gives rewards, but the policy update needs an estimate of whether each sampled action was better or worse than expected. For a terminal segment, compute the discounted return Gt = Rt + γGt+1, then subtract the value baseline: Ât = Gt − V(st).

08 · The update loop

Reuse one fresh batch for bounded passes.

  1. Each of N parallel actors runs πθold for T timesteps.
  2. Compute Ât for every transition, forming N·T samples.
  3. Split that fixed batch into minibatches of size M ≤ N·T.
  4. For K epochs, optimize the clipped actor–critic objective on those minibatches.
  5. Set θold ← θ, then collect a fresh batch.

This is Algorithm 1 in the paper. It is bounded on-policy reuse, not an off-policy replay buffer.

09 · Diagnostics

Watch the update, not only the reward.

Approximate KL
mean[log πold(a|s) − log πθ(a|s)]
Clip fraction
fraction where (Â > 0 and ρ > 1 + ε) or (Â < 0 and ρ < 1 − ε)
Entropy
mean policy entropy (S[π_θ](s)), a read on exploration
Value loss
mean ((V_θ(s)−V^{target})^2), the paper’s (L_t^{VF})

These are practical monitors: a large KL or clip fraction suggests an aggressive update, while collapsing entropy suggests reduced exploration. They do not replace return evaluation.

11 · Limits

A clipped objective is not a formal trust region.

PPO borrows the stabilizing intention of TRPO but does not enforce TRPO’s exact KL-divergence constraint. ε, the number of optimization epochs, reward scaling, and advantage estimation still matter. Because PPO is on-policy, samples become stale as the policy moves; clipping lets the method reuse a batch for several epochs, not indefinitely. The paper’s evidence spans simulated locomotion and Atari benchmarks, not a universal monotonic-improvement guarantee.

12 · Active recall

Can you state the safety rule?

What does rt measure?

The new policy’s probability of the sampled action divided by the old policy’s probability of that same action in that state.

Why does the sign of advantage matter?

Positive advantage supports increasing probability; negative advantage supports decreasing it. The lower-bound objective clips only the direction that would otherwise make the sampled surrogate look overly good.

How does PPO differ from DQN?

DQN learns action values and chooses high-value actions. PPO directly optimizes a stochastic policy’s action probabilities using trajectory advantages.

13 · Connect it to your mental map

From choosing values to shaping behavior.

DQN turns temporal-difference errors into action values; AlphaGo combines policy and value predictions with search. PPO addresses a different training question: how can the parameters of a stochastic policy move repeatedly over one experience batch without letting a local advantage estimate justify an unlimited shift? ReAct is the inference-time loop of reasoning, acting, and observing; PPO is one way an action policy can be trained to change conservatively.

14 · Takeaway

Trust a good trajectory—but only up to a point.

PPO converts “do more of what worked” into a bounded probability-ratio objective. Its clipped surrogate preserves a useful first-order update while refusing to keep rewarding a policy move after it has strayed too far from the policy that generated the evidence.
