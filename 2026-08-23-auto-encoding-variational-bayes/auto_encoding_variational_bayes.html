Learning a map you can sample.

A variational autoencoder does not merely compress an example. It learns where that example could live on a simple latent map - and how to draw a plausible example from any sensible point on that map.

Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes · Diederik P. Kingma and Max Welling · ICLR 2014

1. The problem behind the name

An ordinary autoencoder can learn image → code → reconstruction. It may reconstruct well, but its codes can be an irregular pile: a decoder only sees the particular locations produced by the encoder. Pick a random point between two unrelated codes, and there is no guarantee that it represents anything meaningful.

Predict before revealing. Suppose one image is encoded at (-8, 3) and another at (12, -5), with no rule about the rest of the plane. Would you trust the decoder to turn (2, -1) into a plausible image?
Answer

Not necessarily. A conventional autoencoder is trained to reconstruct codes it produces, not arbitrary points between them. A VAE adds a probabilistic rule that makes the latent space easier to sample from.

AEVB, the paper’s name for the training method, starts from a generative story: first draw an unobserved cause z from a simple prior; then let a decoder use z to explain an observed example x. The difficulty is reversing that story: after seeing x, which z could have caused it?

Bridge to the method. To make a latent map sampleable, we need both directions: a decoder that runs the story forward from a chosen coordinate, and an encoder that makes a sensible guess about coordinates after seeing an example. The next section names those two jobs before we ask how to train them.

Why this enables the objective. Now we have a concrete proposal for the otherwise hard question “which hidden cause made x?”: use qφ(z|x). That proposal lets us score reconstruction, but it also needs a rule that keeps different examples on one map we can sample from.

3. The ELBO is a two-part contract

The exact probability that the model assigns to an example, log pθ(x), is hard to optimize because it requires considering many possible hidden causes z. Think of trying to explain one photograph while considering every possible scene that might have produced it: the forward story is simple, but summing every hidden explanation is not. The paper therefore optimizes a lower bound instead:

L(θ,φ;x)=−DKL(qφ(z|x)∥p(z))+Eqφ(z|x)[logpθ(x|z)]Paper Eq. 3, written as a quantity to maximize.

Reconstruction term

“Can the decoder explain x from sampled z?” Maximizing it rewards faithful reconstruction.

KL term

“Does the encoder’s cloud remain reasonably close to the agreed prior map?” Because it is subtracted, drifting too far costs objective value.

Read the ELBO as one contract, not two unrelated chores: use enough latent information to explain this example, but pay for information that makes the shared map hard to sample. Reconstruction alone would allow an irregular private code for every image; KL alone would make every image look like the same prior cloud. Their tension creates a usable latent space.

Decision exercise. A one-dimensional Gaussian encoder has q(z|x)=N(μ,σ²) while the prior is N(0,1). Its KL cost is ½(μ² + σ² − log σ² − 1). Predict which has zero KL: (μ,σ)=(0,1), (2,1), or (0,0.5).
Work it out

(0,1) has KL 0 because it matches the prior exactly. (2,1) has KL 2: the cloud is shifted. (0,0.5) has positive KL (about 0.32): it is too narrow. This does not mean every encoding should be identical to the prior; reconstruction must be good enough to justify using information in z.

Since we maximize the ELBO, −3 is better than −5. The point is not “minimize both columns independently”; it is to balance them. We can now state the remaining obstacle: the reconstruction score depends on a sampled z, so how can its learning signal reach the encoder’s μ and σ?

4. Move the dice outside the network

Sampling seems to block an ordinary gradient path: how should the encoder learn if the code is written only as z ∼ N(μ, σ²)? The paper rewrites the same sample as:

z=μ+σ⊙ε,ε∼N(0,I)The Gaussian instance of the paper’s general reparameterization z = gφ(ε, x).

The encoder controls μ and σ. The random draw ε comes from a fixed standard-normal source. Once a draw is fixed for one update, the computation from μ, σ to z is ordinary differentiable arithmetic. It is like moving the dice roll to the input of a calculator: the result still varies between rolls, but for this roll you can see exactly how turning μ or σ changes z.

Reparameterization trace

Choose a fixed noise draw and inspect the resulting code. The diagram is useful even without interaction: with μ=2, σ=0.5, and ε=2, the sampled code is 3.

Trace it yourself. If μ=3, σ=0.5, and the fixed draw is ε=−1, calculate z. If the decoder’s reconstruction is poor, which outputs can the encoder adjust?
Answer

z = 3 + 0.5 × (−1) = 2.5. The encoder can adjust its predicted μ and σ. The key benefit is not that probability disappears; it is that continuous randomness is expressed through a differentiable transformation with a low-variance gradient estimator.

Complete training loop. Encode x into μ,σ; draw ε; form z; decode it; score reconstruction and the KL cost; then send gradients back through that arithmetic. This is the bridge from a probabilistic story to ordinary gradient-based learning.

5. What the paper demonstrated - and did not

Kingma and Welling tested image models on MNIST and Frey Face data. Their Figure 2 reports that AEVB converged considerably faster and reached a better variational lower bound than wake-sleep in all displayed settings. Their Figure 3 compares AEVB with wake-sleep and Monte Carlo EM on MNIST; the paper notes that Monte Carlo EM is not an online method and cannot be efficiently applied to the full MNIST dataset.

Read the scope carefully. These are results on the paper’s 2014 benchmarks and architectures, not a claim about modern image-generation quality. The method focuses on continuous latent variables and commonly uses a diagonal-Gaussian approximate posterior. It makes inference tractable; it does not guarantee sharp samples or perfectly disentangled factors.

Common misconceptions

“A VAE is just an autoencoder with noise.”

No. The essential change is probabilistic: an encoder distribution, a simple prior, and the ELBO’s reconstruction-plus-KL objective make sampling from latent space meaningful.

“KL forces every input to encode as zero.”

No. It discourages unearned deviation from the prior. Reconstruction pushes different inputs apart when information is needed.

“The ELBO equals the exact data likelihood.”

No. It is a lower bound. The gap is the KL divergence between the approximate and true posterior.

6. Connect it to your mental map

With GANs: both begin from latent variables and can generate samples. A GAN learns through a discriminator’s adversarial signal; a VAE learns a likelihood-based lower bound with an explicit approximate-posterior encoder. Do not treat their objectives as interchangeable.

With Adam: the reparameterized estimator turns a probabilistic-inference objective into gradients that standard stochastic optimizers can use. Adam changes how those gradients are scaled; AEVB changes how the gradients become available.

With Batch Normalization and ResNets: all are influential training ideas, but their bottlenecks differ. Batch normalization manages activation statistics; residual connections ease optimization flow; a VAE makes latent-variable inference tractable enough for gradient learning.

With DQN and DPO: those learn from reward or preference signals. A VAE is neither reward learning nor preference learning: it learns a generative explanation of observed data through hidden variables.

7. Active recall

Why is a simple prior useful?

It gives a known region from which we can sample. The KL term encourages encoded examples to occupy a latent space that is compatible with that sampling rule.

What is the encoder’s output for a Gaussian VAE?

Parameters such as μ(x) and σ(x) for an approximate posterior distribution, not only one deterministic code.

What does the reparameterization trick rewrite?

A sample from a parameterized distribution as a deterministic transformation of parameter-free noise: z = μ + σ ⊙ ε.

What two pressures does the ELBO balance?

Faithfully reconstructing/explaining the data and keeping the approximate posterior reasonably close to the prior.

Takeaway

A VAE learns a decoder that can generate from a simple latent map and an encoder that places each observation on that map as a probability cloud. The ELBO balances faithful reconstruction with keeping the map sampleable; reparameterization makes that balance trainable with gradients.
