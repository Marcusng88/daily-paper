Adaptive optimization · Kingma & Ba

One update, two memories.

Adam does not choose a direction from today’s gradient alone. It keeps a memory of signed direction and a separate memory of typical gradient scale, then uses both to calibrate each parameter’s next step.

16–30 minTrace two updates. Calculate bias correction and see what happens when the gradient reverses.

01 A global knob meets uneven terrain

Plain stochastic gradient descent uses one rule for every parameter: θt=θt−1−αgt. The gradient says which way locally reduces the current minibatch loss; α says how far to trust it. But parameters can have radically different gradient scales. A learning rate that is cautious for one coordinate can be violent for another.

Momentum

Remember a smoothed signed direction. Consistent gradients reinforce one another; noisy reversals are softened.

RMS-style scaling

Remember recent squared magnitude. A coordinate with persistently large gradients receives a smaller normalized step.

Adam

Keep both memories, correct their early underestimation, then take a coordinatewise update.

Prerequisite bridge. Gradient descent is not a model architecture. It is the procedure that changes every learned weight while Transformer attention, ResNet blocks, DQN values, GAN players, and BatchNorm parameters are trained.

02 Map the symbols to jobs

Bridge from the problem to the method. One global knob cannot respond separately to a parameter that keeps receiving large slopes and one that receives tiny, noisy slopes. Adam therefore gives every parameter two short ledgers: one records direction; the other records typical size. The equations below are just the bookkeeping rule for those two ledgers and the step they authorize.

03 The first two steps, by hand

Why this comes next. The symbols can look like one large formula until we watch a ledger start empty and then receive conflicting evidence. This single-parameter trace turns the two memories into ordinary arithmetic before we generalize back to a whole network.

Use one scalar parameter, m0=v0=0, β1=0.9, β2=0.999, and α=0.001. Ignore ε only to keep the arithmetic readable.

Trace A: the first gradient is +4

m1=0.9(0)+0.1(4)=0.4    v1=0.999(0)+0.001(4²)=0.016
m̂1=0.41−0.9=4v̂1=0.0161−0.999=16Δθ1=−0.001·416=−0.001

Without correction, the fresh memories are too small because they began at zero. At this first step, bias correction exactly restores the observed gradient and its square.

Trace B: the next gradient reverses to −2

m2=0.9(0.4)+0.1(−2)=0.16    m̂2≈0.842
v2=0.999(0.016)+0.001(4)=0.019984    v̂2≈9.997
Δθ2≈−0.001·0.8429.997≈−0.000266

The current gradient is negative, but the smoothed direction is still positive. Adam therefore continues a smaller positive-direction descent step in this sign convention. This is not a mistake: it is the explicit trade-off made by the direction memory.

Predict before reveal: should the second update flip?

After +4 then −2, does Adam’s second update immediately follow the new negative gradient, or does its direction history still matter?

Static answer: History still matters. The corrected first moment remains positive (about 0.842), so the second parameter step is still negative in the update formula, but much smaller than α.

04 One learning rate, coordinatewise calibration

What the trace enabled. We saw the signed ledger decide the direction and the squared ledger temper the step. Now hold the same rule against two coordinates with radically different raw scales; this is the reason Adam’s two ledgers matter beyond one scalar toy example.

Now imagine two coordinates at the first step: g=[10, 0.1]. With ε negligible, first-step bias correction gives m̂=g and √v̂=|g|. Adam’s update is approximately −α[1,1] for two positive coordinates, while raw SGD would take steps in a 100:1 magnitude ratio.

SGD−αg=[−10α,−0.1α]. The large-gradient coordinate dominates the physical step.
Adam−αg/|g|≈[−α,−α]. Per-coordinate normalization balances initial scale; ε protects division near zero.
Do not overstate it. Squared-gradient scaling is diagonal and historical. It is not a full Hessian, it does not know the best learning-rate schedule, and it does not guarantee the best validation performance.
Diagnostic: is vt the variance of gradients?

No. It is an exponential moving average of uncentred squared gradients. The paper calls it a second raw moment estimate; it retains magnitude information, not deviation around a mean.

Diagnostic: are the bias corrections repairing biased gradients?

No. They repair the moment estimates’ initialization bias. Starting an EMA at zero makes its early magnitude systematically too small even if the incoming stochastic gradients themselves are unbiased estimates.

05 What the paper establishes—and what it does not

The paper evaluates Adam on L2-regularized logistic regression, multilayer neural networks, and convolutional neural networks. On MNIST logistic regression, it reports Adam converging similarly to SGD with Nesterov momentum and faster than AdaGrad; on sparse IMDB bag-of-words features, Adam converges as fast as AdaGrad. Its neural-network and CNN comparisons are empirical, not covered by the paper’s convex-online convergence analysis.

Useful default, not a law

For the tested machine-learning problems, Algorithm 1 gives α=0.001, β1=0.9, β2=0.999, and ε=10−8 as good default settings.

A scoped theoretical claim

The convergence section works in online convex optimization with stated boundedness and decay assumptions. It should not be read as a proof that Adam converges best on arbitrary modern deep networks.

For CIFAR-10 CNNs, the paper reports Adam and SGD eventually converging considerably faster than AdaGrad in its displayed experiment, with Adam showing marginal improvement over SGD with momentum while adapting scales across layers. That is evidence for those architectures, hyperparameter searches, and training costs—not a universal optimizer ranking.

06 Active recall

  1. Why do zero-initialized m and v start too small?
  2. If coordinate A’s first gradient is 100 times coordinate B’s, are their first Adam update magnitudes also 100 times apart when ε is negligible?
  3. What does m remember that v does not?
  4. Why can Adam still move according to earlier positive gradients after a new negative gradient appears?
  5. What must evidence for a new optimizer establish?
Open the answer key
  1. Each exponential average begins with no accumulated history, so it contains only a fraction of the early signal. Dividing by 1−βt corrects that initialization shrinkage.
  2. Roughly no. At the first step, g/|g| has unit magnitude for each nonzero coordinate, so both are near α in magnitude before ε matters.
  3. m remembers signed direction; v remembers recent squared magnitude and loses sign.
  4. The first-moment average retains weighted positive history, so it need not reverse as soon as one gradient sample reverses.
  5. It must establish behavior on stated tasks and conditions, plus any assumptions behind theory; it cannot justify a universal “best optimizer” claim.

07 Connect it to your mental map

Transformer
Attention routes information between token representations. Adam is a common way to train those parameters: it turns a history of noisy gradients into calibrated coordinate steps, while the Transformer’s own schedule remains a separate design decision.
ResNet
Residual shortcuts preserve a baseline representation and an additive gradient route through depth. Adam does not create that route; it decides how large each weight update along the optimizer’s route should be.
DQN
DQN uses replay and a target network to control correlated data and moving bootstrap labels. Adam controls uneven, noisy parameter updates. These stabilize different failure modes.
BatchNorm
BatchNorm manages activation scale inside the network; Adam manages parameter-update scale across optimization time. Do not confuse a normalized feature with a normalized optimizer step.
GAN
Adam can adapt the generator’s and discriminator’s individual steps, but two adaptive optimizers do not eliminate the game’s coupled, non-stationary dynamics or mode collapse.

Takeaway

Native intuition: Adam keeps two imperfect ledgers for every parameter: where gradients have tended to point, and how large they have tended to be. Bias correction stops the empty ledgers from lying at the start. The resulting step is not “the right answer”; it is a measured next move, scaled separately for each coordinate.
